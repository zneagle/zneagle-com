01What you’re looking at

PAC is currently in its smallest possible form: a deliberately bounded experimental machine built to make a few distinctions observable and testable.

The live demonstrator uses five deterministic scenarios to separate authority, safety, execution, evidence uncertainty, and consumed-authorization refusal. The robot work cell and upstream proposal are simulated. No external AI model is connected, and this is not a production safety system.

Where I intend to take it — proposal, not available. The apparatus described here does not exist yet.

The next research direction is to expand this small apparatus into a researcher-facing experimental machine. A researcher could connect a model or agent, define a task and explicit human constraints, and observe what happens as proposed actions pass through the same authority boundary into a controlled action environment.

The machine would preserve the path from:

human intention → machine interpretation → proposed action → authority → attempted action → resulting state → evidence

The purpose is not to prove that AI misbehaves. It is to build an apparatus capable of distinguishing machine error, human specification error, authority error, execution error, evidence failure, and cases where the available evidence is insufficient to determine what happened.

02What PAC explores

PAC began as an experiment in separating one question from two others: whether a machine has legitimate, current authority for a specific physical action, as distinct from whether it can perform that action and whether the action is independently safe. It is a research demonstrator, not a production safety system, a proposed industry standard, or a deployed industrial control architecture.

That boundary remains useful. The current research direction is increasingly focused on what the boundary can make observable. When human authority and intention are represented explicitly enough to test, machine behavior can be measured against them, and the boundary becomes an experimental reference surface. The question is no longer only “can PAC authorize this correctly?” but also “what kinds of machine error become visible when authority and intention are explicit enough to check?”

The replay implementation, tests, and documentation in this release were developed with AI assistance under my direction and review. The source and checks make that work available for inspection within the demonstrator's stated limits. I present it for review of:

  • Systems reasoning
  • Separating authority from safety
  • Falsifiability
  • Adversarial review
  • Narrowing claims under evidence
  • Technical research discipline

03Three separate questions

  1. CapabilityCan it?Can the system understand the situation and perform the action?
  2. AuthorityMay it, now?Does legitimate, current authority presently exist for this specific action? This is PAC’s question.
  3. SafetyIs it safe?May the machine physically execute under its safety constraints? A PAC permit does not override independent safety.

04RoboBoston demonstrator

The event demonstration uses a simulated test-cell valve scenario. No physical actuator is connected. Five public scenarios have been exercised on the deployed demonstrator, deliberately kept distinct rather than collapsed into a generic "blocked" state:

  • D01 — Authorized actionPAC returns PERMIT, independent safety returns ALLOW, and the simulated action reaches EXECUTED.
  • D02 — Missing authorityRequired institutional work permission is missing. PAC returns DENY and no execution occurs.
  • D03 — Consumed authorityA previously consumed authorization instance is replayed without re-adjudication. Execution is refused as AUTHORITY_CONSUMED. This demonstrates refusal of the exact consumed instance — it does not prove equivalent authority can never be separately reissued; that remains an open research question.
  • D04 — Independent safety vetoPAC returns PERMIT, but independent safety returns DENY. Execution is refused as NO_EXECUTION:SAFETY_DENY, no execution occurs, and the authorization remains unconsumed. In this demo the safety input is simulated. PAC treats safety as a distinct gate; this does not demonstrate a separate certified or independently deployed safety subsystem.
  • D05 — Unknown mandatory evidenceMandatory asset identity is UNKNOWN. PAC returns DEFER, no authorization is issued, and no execution occurs. [OASIS]

D01–D05 is the first bounded experimental apparatus: a small, simulated environment in which permit, authority denial, consumed-authority refusal, safety denial, and epistemic defer — refusing to decide because required evidence is unknown — stay distinguishable. Those explicit states are what later model-error experiments would be measured against. The apparatus does not establish that PAC is novel or necessary, that it should become a standard, or that it is safe for production use.

06From authority to error

The research target is the gap between what a person intended and what a machine-mediated system actually did. The intent is to give each step a place where it can be recorded and compared:

human intention → machine interpretation → proposed action → permitted authority → attempted action → resulting state → recorded evidence

Cases of interest are described as observed system behavior, not intent. They include outputs or actions that:

  • preserve the imposed authority correctly — the compliant baseline;
  • conflict with it, or route around it;
  • exploit ambiguity in how it is represented;
  • expand or narrow the apparent instruction;
  • replay or reuse something that should no longer authorize action;
  • reach an apparently successful result by an unintended path;
  • report completion that the available evidence does not support;
  • or reveal an error class that was not anticipated.

“Authority violation” here means an observed discrepancy against an explicit experimental definition. It says nothing about what a model wanted, chose, or understood.

Adversarial review has raised the possibility that substantial parts of the current D01–D05 behavior may be reproducible using conventional authorization, capability, workflow, audit, and safety mechanisms. That redundancy question remains unresolved.

07Where a discrepancy comes from

An unexpected result is not automatically model misbehavior. A discrepancy would be classified before it is attributed:

  • Human specificationThe human constraint failed to represent what the human actually intended.
  • TranslationIntent changed when represented in prompts, structured state, plans, or commands.
  • Model outputThe generated proposal or output conflicts with the represented constraint.
  • AuthorityThe authority machinery issued, denied, consumed, retained, or interpreted authority incorrectly.
  • ExecutionThe commanded operation and the actual operation diverged.
  • EvidenceThe recorded account of what happened is incomplete, misleading, stale, or wrong.
  • Uncertain completionThe system cannot establish whether the consequential physical action actually completed.
  • UnknownThe evidence does not justify assigning the discrepancy to any one class.

Classification is meant to be part of the experiment: a result that looks like model error may turn out to be an error in the human specification, or in PAC itself.

08Why study errors?

Unexpected model behavior already appears across current AI evaluation research, under different names and experimental definitions. The examples below motivate preserving such behavior carefully. None of their authors endorse PAC, PAC did not discover these phenomena, and PAC’s discrepancy classes are not their definitions.

  • Controlled evaluationsOpenAI, with Apollo Research (17 Sep 2025), reported behaviors consistent with scheming in controlled tests of several frontier models, and a training method that reduced but did not eliminate them. OpenAI states it has no evidence that today’s deployed frontier models could suddenly begin significantly harmful scheming. [OpenAI/Apollo]
  • Stress testsAnthropic (20 Jun 2025) stress-tested 16 models in constructed scenarios involving goal conflicts or threatened replacement; some models took harmful actions such as blackmail. Anthropic describes these as controlled simulations and states it has not seen evidence of agentic misalignment in real deployments. [Anthropic]
  • Specification gamingBondarenko et al. (preprint, Feb 2025, revised Aug 2025) asked LLM agents to win against a chess engine; some reasoning models often “hack the benchmark” instead of playing, satisfying the stated goal by an unintended path. [arXiv]
  • Disclosure practiceOpenAI (16 Sep 2026) published a framework for tracking, investigating and disclosing model misalignment across training, evaluation, testing and deployment, noting that individual reports do not indicate how often such behavior occurs. [OpenAI]

Together they suggest that preserving comparable, well-evidenced examples of machine-behavior failure is itself an open research problem. PAC’s proposed corpus is one attempt to work on that problem; it does not claim to solve it.

09Proposed next research direction

Proposal — not available. The research service described here does not exist yet in this multi-user, API-routed form.

The intended next step is a researcher-accessible experimental service. A researcher would connect an AI model or agent through an API and route it through the same bounded apparatus used for other models:

researcher → model / agent API → task + explicit human constraints → PAC experimental authority boundary → controlled action environment → observation → artifact / error record

It is not intended as a model leaderboard. The aim is reproducible examples of discrepancy between explicit human intention or authority and machine behavior.

Variables a researcher should eventually be able to change:

  • Models
  • Model providers
  • Prompts
  • Agents
  • Planning architectures
  • Tool configurations
  • Tasks
  • Authority structures
  • Retries
  • Evidence conditions

10The intended corpus

The intended output is a structured corpus of machine-error artifacts. Each useful case would keep enough evidence to reconstruct:

  1. the human instruction or intention;
  2. the explicit authority and constraints;
  3. the relevant machine input and context;
  4. the model output or proposed action;
  5. the authority evaluation;
  6. the safety state, where applicable;
  7. the attempted or executed action;
  8. the resulting state;
  9. the discrepancy classification;
  10. reproducibility information.

Positive and negative cases both belong in it: compliant runs, failures, false positives, rejected incidents, ambiguous cases, and cases that could not be reproduced. Without denominators and negative cases it would be a collection of anecdotes, not empirical research.

Questions it is meant to help ask — not questions PAC already answers:

  • Which errors recur across models, and which are model-specific?
  • Which arise from ambiguous human specification, or from translation between natural language and machine state?
  • Which resemble specification gaming, and which involve misleading completion or evidence?
  • Which involve the authority lifecycle or replay?
  • Which disappear when constraints are represented differently, and which mitigations actually reduce recurrence?
  • Which “failures” turn out to be mistakes in our own specification?

If enough comparable examples accumulate, the value would be understanding recurring failure modes well enough to design better experiments, interfaces, constraints, evaluations, and mitigations.

11What is not proven

PAC is intentionally incomplete. Open or unvalidated areas include:

  • A01Authority-independence semantics.Open question
  • A02Equivalent-authority reissuance / duplicate issuance / double-spend semantics.Open question
  • A03K_OF_N.k semantics.Open question
  • Error studyNo external AI model has been connected to PAC; the demonstrator's upstream proposal is scripted. The research service and corpus in sections 09–10 are proposals, and no model-error corpus has been collected.Open question
  • Distributed / physicalPersistence and restart behavior; concurrency and split brain; revocation races; stale evidence [W3C]; delegation authenticity; RPC identity; uncertainty about whether a physical action actually completed; distributed correctness; production reliability and availability.Open question

No physical robot or actuator has been controlled by the RoboBoston demonstrator. The project does not claim certification, production safety, distributed correctness, or proven novelty.

12Prior art / redundancy pressure

PAC overlaps conceptually with established areas including IAM / RBAC / ABAC [NIST], capability-based security, Zero Trust-style per-resource authorization [NIST], usage control, workflow and permit-to-work systems [HSE], functional safety, industrial-control access authorization [OPC UA], robotics middleware authorization [ROS 2], and OT security. That overlap is a research constraint, not something to hide. The strongest question is whether "current, action-specific physical authority" deserves to remain an explicit decision boundary or whether the same semantics should live entirely inside existing authorization, workflow, safety, or orchestration systems.

13What would falsify the useful-core claim

Useful expert challenges include:

  1. Show an existing robotics or industrial-control architecture that already provides the same machine/action/target/current-evidence authority boundary without collapsing it into capability or safety.
  2. Show that authority is not a useful separate primitive and that PAC adds only redundant machinery.
  3. Identify the smallest distributed or physical-world failure mode that destroys the claimed boundary.

PAC is intended to survive those questions or become narrower because of them.

14And the business model?

Currently: keep the machine alive.

I don’t have much interest in restricting access to observations about AI error simply to create something to sell. If PAC can expand the surface area of what we can empirically understand about failures between human intention, machine behavior, authority, action, and evidence, my preference is to make that research broadly useful.

The practical requirement is less glamorous: sustenance funding and infrastructure. Enough support for the experimental machine to operate reliably, grow, preserve its evidence, and increasingly run without requiring my constant attention — so I can keep working on the next problem.

If someone eventually discovers an excellent business model for “please fund the weird machine so the weird machine can keep finding interesting errors,” I am receptive.

15Disclosure

Experimental research demonstrator. PAC is not presented as a production safety system, certified control standard, proposed industry standard, deployed autonomous authority mechanism, or proof of novelty. The research service and corpus described above are proposals and do not yet exist.

STANDARD eXtensible Access Control Markup Language (XACML) Version 3.0 (2013)

OASIS Standard

Why it matters XACML distinguishes NotApplicable (no policy applies) from Indeterminate (evaluation failed or conflicted) as separate decision outcomes. PAC's D05 independently returns DEFER for unknown mandatory evidence; the two are not claimed to be equivalent, and this source is not evidence that PAC does or does not match that distinction — that question can only be answered from PAC's own artifacts. XACML is offered here as relevant prior art for thinking about differentiated authorization outcomes.

Supports XACML's differentiated policy-decision outcomes, as prior art for authorization-outcome semantics — not a claim about PAC's own implementation.

Why it matters Published 17 September 2025. NIST SPECIAL PUBLICATION Guide to Attribute Based Access Control (ABAC) Definition and Considerations (2014, upd2 2019-08-02 (current active edition))

V. Hu et al., NIST

Why it matters The canonical NIST definition of ABAC — authorization decided by evaluating subject/object/action/environment attributes against policy — is the closest mainstream analog to what PAC evaluates.

NIST SPECIAL PUBLICATION Zero Trust Architecture (2020)

S. Rose, O. Borchert, S. Mitchell, S. Connelly, NIST

Why it matters Zero Trust treats authentication and authorization as discrete decisions made per session against a specific resource, rather than granted by network location — an established authorization-architecture alternative to perimeter trust. It is not evidence about capability-based security, and the document does not describe a per-action authorization model; the comparison is limited to that authorization framing.

Supports Zero Trust's per-resource, per-session authorization model, as an alternative to perimeter trust.

OFFICIAL GUIDANCE Permit-to-work systems (current guidance)

UK Health and Safety Executive

Why it matters States the exact distinction PAC's own architecture depends on, in an unrelated, decades-old industrial-safety domain: “the issue of a permit does not, by itself, make a job safe.” Also the clearest limit-case for PAC's analogy, since a paper permit assumes a competent human, not an autonomous executor.

Supports Authorization is not the same thing as safety.

STANDARD OPC Unified Architecture, Part 3: Address Space Model — RolePermissions / UserRolePermissions / AccessRestrictions (5.2.9–5.2.11) (current release)

OPC Foundation

Why it matters An existing, widely deployed industrial-automation standard already attaches role-based permissions and access restrictions directly to individual nodes — a mature, narrower-scoped precedent for action-level authorization. This card is about that authorization mechanism specifically; it is not a functional-safety standard and establishes no functional-safety prior art.

Supports Industrial-automation node-level access-control authorization — not functional safety.
