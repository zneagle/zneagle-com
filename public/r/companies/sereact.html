04Individualized letter

Sereact Hardware Technician (US) cover letter, 2026-09-17.

Read the full letter

Dear Sereact Hiring Team,

I did not arrive at robotics by deciding to leave behind the technical work I had already learned. I arrived there because, after years of moving through increasingly complex systems, those different disciplines kept revealing the same underlying structure.

Audio engineering was where that structure first became explicit to me. Working with signal chains taught me to stop seeing equipment as isolated devices and start seeing systems as paths: sources feeding transformations, control signals determining behavior, interfaces handing information from one stage to the next, and routing decisions creating consequences far downstream. Troubleshooting became an exercise in topology. Where did the signal originate? What acted on it? What controlled its path? Which dependency failed first? What should have happened next?

That way of thinking followed me everywhere else.

Mechanical and automotive work taught me to trace physical causality through engines, drivetrains, electrical faults, and failure modes. Metal fabrication and welding taught me that a design eventually has to survive material reality: fit, tolerance, sequence, tooling, access, heat, force, and the difference between what should work and what actually does. Audio, fabrication, mechanical diagnostics, and field work looked like separate interests from the outside, but they were steadily training the same habit of mind.

The pattern became even clearer in later technical environments. At Level99, I worked around interactive systems combining RFID and other sensors, PLC-monitored elements, DMX, Arduino hardware, programmable LEDs, audio, mechanical components, and computer control. At the Starlink facility in Bastrop, Texas, I saw advanced industrial systems operating at a scale where integration, sequencing, reliability, and infrastructure were inseparable. I later completed the Journeyman One program at Martin Electrical School, where I was one of two students in my class to score 100 on the midterm examination, adding formal electrical theory and safety to a foundation that had already been built through years of practical systems work.

My work history was not a collection of unrelated jobs. Each domain had been adding another layer to the same technical foundation.

That is why robotics now feels less like a career change than a convergence point. A robot is simultaneously a mechanical structure, an electrical system, a sensor network, a control architecture, a software system, and a physical actor whose outputs have consequences in the world. It is exactly the kind of system I have been moving toward from several directions at once.

I am not presenting myself as an experienced robotics engineer, embedded-firmware specialist, or someone who has already mastered every layer of that stack. What I can bring immediately is proven fabrication experience, mechanical troubleshooting, comfort with tools and equipment, electrical fundamentals, practical systems judgment, and a habit of tracing consequential signal and control paths across disciplines.

That is what makes Sereact's Hardware Technician role especially compelling to me. Building and maintaining robotic stations, fabricating parts and fixtures, supporting hardware modifications, preparing systems for deployment, and troubleshooting mechanical and electrical problems all sit directly at the intersection between what I can already do and what I am deliberately trying to become better at.

I would welcome the opportunity to discuss how that background could contribute to Sereact's Boston hardware team. Thank you for taking the time to consider this, and for whatever we get to discuss at RoboBoston.

Sincerely,

Zachary Neagle
